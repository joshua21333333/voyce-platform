The problem isn't that AI writes badly. It's that AI writes for the average.

Every model trains on what already exists. It learns what a "good LinkedIn post" looks like by ingesting thousands of them. Then it reproduces the pattern. Confident opener. Three supporting points. Call to action. Repeat.

That's not your voice. That's the statistical center of everyone's voice.

When you prompt an AI with "write a post about our product launch," it doesn't know what you'd actually say if someone asked you about it over coffee. It doesn't know you'd skip the feature list and go straight to the customer problem you almost got wrong. It doesn't know you'd use that specific word you always use, or make that slight detour into a related observation that somehow makes the main point land harder.

It only knows what most people have written about most product launches.

So it gives you that.

The output isn't wrong. It's just no one's. It could have come from any company in your category, any founder in your space, any agency that's never spoken to you.

The fix isn't a better prompt. It's a different input.

AI produces averaged content because you're feeding it averaged instructions. "Write like me" is not a training set. Your word choices, your opinions, your specific way of explaining things — that's the raw material. Without it, the model defaults to the mean.

Give it evidence of who you actually are, and the output changes. Not because the model got smarter. Because you stopped asking it to guess.
